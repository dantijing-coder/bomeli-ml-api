================================================================================
1. THE ONE RULE BEHIND EVERYTHING
================================================================================

  TRAIN ON PRACTICE DATA, USE ON REAL DATA.

  - Every model learns from SIMULATED ("practice") data that we generated to
    look like a Philippine motorcycle-financing business: thousands of
    borrowers, payments, stock movements and towns.
  - The models are then run on our REAL records (MySQL) to make predictions.
  - Real records are used to CHECK the models, never to teach them.

  Why: our real history is still small (a few hundred accounts, three
  branches). A model that learns from so little just memorises it. Practice
  data gives the model enough examples to learn the general patterns (e.g.
  "people with a high debt-to-income ratio miss more payments"). Where the
  practice data's scale does not match ours (for example simulated towns
  producing many more clients), we rescale the output to our real numbers.
  M3 and M7 do this explicitly.

  Where you can re-train:
    train_models.py            -> M1, M2, M4, M5 (and the unused M6 classifiers)
    train_velocity_model.py    -> M3
    train_sales_forecast_model.py -> M8 (horizon sales forecast)
    train_branch_expansion.py  -> M7 (also re-run automatically after every
                                  "Run AI" and after the history replay)

================================================================================
3. WHY TREE-BASED MODELS (AND NOT NEURAL NETWORKS)
================================================================================

  Almost every learned model here is a "gradient-boosted decision tree"
  model. In plain words: a long list of small yes/no rule trees ("Is
  debt-to-income above 35%? Are 2+ terms overdue?"), where each new tree fixes
  the mistakes of the ones before it.

  Why we chose it:
  1. Our data is a table (rows = borrowers, columns = income, overdue terms,
     etc.). On table data, tree models are usually as accurate as neural
     networks, or more accurate, and much simpler to run.
  2. It handles mixed data (numbers + categories like "OFW" or "Rented")
     without heavy preparation, and it copes with missing values.
  3. It picks up "combination" effects on its own (high debt AND short time
     at address is worse than either alone).
  4. It is small and fast: all models together load in a few MB and score
     the whole portfolio in well under a second on an ordinary server. No
     graphics card needed.
  5. It can be explained: we can show which inputs push a prediction up or
     down.

  Technical name: HistGradientBoostingClassifier / HistGradientBoostingRegressor
  (scikit-learn).

  The only exception is the town-demand part of M7, which uses a simpler
  "counting" formula (see M7) because it has very few rows to learn from.

M1. DEFAULT RISK - "Will this borrower stop paying in the next 90 days?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Risk tab -> Account Watchlist (the risk % per borrower), the priority
         badges, and the action buttons next to each account.

  What it looks at (per borrower):
    - how far into the loan they are (terms paid / total terms)
    - debt-to-income (monthly amortization / monthly income)
    - on-time streak (months in a row paid on time, earning the rebate)
    - late streak and number of terms currently overdue
    - how often they pay only part of the amount
    - on-time reliability (share of due dates paid on time)
    - credit-investigation red flags, unreachable phone number
    - years at current address
    - job type (employed, self-employed, OFW, transport)
    - home ownership (owned, mortgaged, rented)

  How it works: the tree model gives each borrower a raw score. A second step
  then adjusts that score so it can be read as a real chance. After this step,
  among borrowers shown as "70%", roughly 7 in 10 would be expected to stop
  paying.

  Why this method:
    - Tree model: reasons in Section 3.
    - The adjustment step: raw tree scores are good at ORDERING borrowers
      (who is riskier) but the numbers themselves are not true percentages.
      Staff act on the percentage, so it has to mean what it says.

  Risk tiers used by "Run AI" (live):
    70% and up  -> Critical -> "Schedule Field Visit"
    45% - 69%   -> High     -> "Send Follow-Up SMS" (with waiver offer)
    20% - 44%   -> Moderate -> "Send Reminder SMS"
    below 20%   -> Low      -> no action
  (The history replay uses 70 / 40 / 15. See Section 8.)

  If the model file is missing: a simple rule by overdue terms
  (3+ -> 85%, 2 -> 55%, 1 -> 25%, 0 -> 1.5%).

  Accuracy (on practice data the model never saw, 700 borrowers):
    85.4% correct | 84.3% balanced (fair to both groups) | ranking score 0.86
    (1.0 = perfect ranking, 0.5 = coin toss)

  Technical name: HistGradientBoostingClassifier + CalibratedClassifierCV
  (sigmoid, 3-fold). File: models/default_hazard_model.joblib

M2. EARLY PAYOFF - "Will this borrower settle the loan early?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Operations tab -> Early Payoff Candidates; "Send Early Payoff Quote".

  Background: under the Option Contract, borrowers who have paid on time can
  settle early with an interest discount. Settling early brings in cash
  sooner, so it pays to know who is likely to do it.

  What it looks at: how far into the loan, on-time reliability,
  debt-to-income, on-time streak, overdue terms, monthly income, job type,
  home ownership.

  How a borrower becomes a candidate:
    - model chance is 65% or more, OR
    - they are in an option window (terms 10-12 or 22-24) with 85%+ on-time
    HIGH if on-time is 92%+, otherwise MEDIUM.
  Candidates are then ranked by a "payoff readiness" number:
    model chance x (0.55 + 0.45 x share of loan already paid) x (0.6 if behind)

  Why the extra ranking step: the model gives many steady payers almost the
  same score. Someone with 3 terms left is far more likely to settle now
  than someone with 20 left, so we weight by progress.

  Why a tree model: same reasons as M1. It was trained with "balanced"
  weighting because early payoffs are rare. Without it the model could
  score well by always saying "no".

  Accuracy (700 unseen practice borrowers):
    88.9% correct | 94.2% balanced | ranking score 0.97

  Technical name: HistGradientBoostingClassifier (class_weight balanced).
  File: models/early_settlement_model.joblib

M3. STOCK SALES PACE - "How many of this model will this branch sell?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Sales & Stock tab -> How Fast Each Model Sells. Trend view: the 4
         best-selling models, last 2 months + this month + 2-month forecast.
         Table view: every model with stock on hand and how long it lasts.
         Also restock / transfer advice in Recommended Actions.

  What it looks at (for one motorcycle model at one branch):
    - units sold last month, last 3 months, last 6 months, same month last year
    - units of this model sold across ALL branches in the last 3 months
    - units of ALL models sold at this branch in the last 3 months (foot traffic)
    - units on the floor at the start of the month
    - price, brand, calendar month

  How it works: predicts the expected number of units sold next month. Then:
    stock lasts about = units in stock / (predicted monthly units / 30) days
    Running out: 7 days or less | Low: 8-21 days | Enough: over 21 days
  A branch low on a model is paired with a branch that has slow-moving stock
  of the same model for a transfer suggestion.

  The 2-month forecast on the trend chart: each forecast month takes M8's
  total sales forecast and splits it across models by each model's share of
  M3's next-month prediction. So the model lines always add up to the Sales
  This Month forecast instead of telling a different story.
  (dml/forecast_helpers.py -> forecast_models_by_month; stored per model as
  forecast_by_month.)

  Why this method (version 2, replaced the old decision-tree version):
    - The old version learned from practice data where a model sold ~13
      units a quarter per branch. We actually sell 0-3. At our scale it
      predicted about 2.2 for almost everything. So:
    - Practice data was rebuilt at OUR scale (~10 units a month for the
      whole network; most model-branch-months sell zero).
    - It uses a "counting" setup made for small whole numbers (0, 1, 2...),
      which never predicts negative sales and handles lots of zeros well.
    - It uses real recent sales (lags) instead of a fixed branch name, so it
      works for any branch, including a new one.

  Accuracy:
    Practice data (6,000 unseen model-months):
      average miss (MAE) 0.24 units per model per branch per month
      (repeating last month's sales misses by about the same, 0.24)
    Real sales history (check only, never used for training, 1,096 rows):
      average miss 0.32 units vs 0.34 for the last-3-month average
    Its bigger advantage is fewer wild misses. Its error score that punishes
    predicting sales where there were none (and missing spikes) is about 4x
    lower than the simple average (practice 0.53 vs 2.06; real 0.79 vs 3.31).

  Technical name: HistGradientBoostingRegressor (loss = Poisson).
  File: models/inventory_velocity_model.joblib (version 2)

M4. COLLECTIONS FORECAST - "How much cash will we actually collect?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Collections tab -> Money Received This Month, Payments: Recent
         Months & Next 2 Months chart, Collected This Month card (top),
         History tab -> Amount Due vs Amount Received.

  How it works (two parts):
    1. The contract schedule: the exact installments due this month, added up
       from installment_schedule in the database. This is the "Due this
       month" figure everywhere on the page. It is fact, not a guess.
       (Fixed 2026-10-01: the live engine used to add up every loan's monthly
       amortization instead. That counted new loans with nothing due yet and
       missed loans with two installments due, e.g. Oct 2026 showed P1,108,136
       instead of the real P1,165,547. Both pipelines now use the schedule.)
       "Could be collected" = the schedule minus installments on units that
       were already repossessed (they will not be paid).
    2. A learned "realization rate": what share of that schedule really comes
       in, and a "stress factor" for the bad case. It is predicted from the
       portfolio's make-up: share of accounts on time / behind / stopped,
       average debt-to-income, average on-time reliability, how far into
       their loans people are, how many accounts are new, recent catch-up
       rate, month of the year, expected sales.
    Expected = schedule x realization rate (kept between 85% and 105%).
    Worst case uses the stress factor (kept between 75% and 95%).
    Next months = booked schedule x realization rate
                  + first installments from motorcycles we expect to sell
                    (the units come from M8's sales forecast).
    Best case +8% and worst case -12% are stored around it.

  Why this method:
    - Starting from the real schedule means the forecast can never invent
      money that is not owed.
    - Collections depend on portfolio health and season (13th-month pay in
      Nov-Dec, tuition dip in June). A fixed "we collect 88%" number misses
      that, so a learned rate replaced the fixed one.

  Payments are sorted into streams by simple rules: regular, advance
  (1.5x+ the amortization), partial (under 85%), early settlement (notes
  mention option contract / buyout).

  If the model is missing: fixed 88% stress factor and rule-based rate.

  Accuracy (800 unseen practice portfolio snapshots):
    realization rate explains 94.4% of the variation, average miss 1.9 points
    stress factor explains 93.8%, average miss 1.5 points

  Technical name: two HistGradientBoostingRegressors (realization + stress).
  File: models/cash_forecast_model.joblib

M5. LOAN LIFECYCLE - "How do 12 / 24 / 36-month loans usually end?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Risk tab -> Multi-term benchmarks, active lifecycle distribution.

  How it works: for each account, a model predicts how the loan will end
  (completed, settled early, or defaulted) and how much of it will be
  completed. Results are averaged per term length (12, 24, 36 months). Each
  account is also placed in a phase: early risk (terms 1-4), option window,
  steady middle, or final stretch.

  Why this method: a fixed table says the same thing for every portfolio.
  Predicting per account means the benchmark reflects OUR borrowers.

  If there are no accounts or the model is missing: a fixed reference table
  (completion 85.4% / 81.6% / 77.8% for 12 / 24 / 36 months).

  Note: the "risk driver" pills (e.g. "Partial payments 3.15x") are fixed
  reference figures written into the code. They are not calculated from our
  data.

  Accuracy (800 unseen practice loans):
    default detector 85.0% correct (85.8% balanced)
    early-settlement detector 72.8% correct
    completion share: explains 78.3% of the variation, average miss 2.6 points

  Technical name: HistGradientBoosting classifiers + regressor.
  File: models/survival_analysis_model.joblib

M6. PAYMENT BEHAVIOR - "How many slip, catch up, or stop paying?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Borrower Health tab -> What To Expect Next Month (3 rows), Branch
         Comparison, and the 2-month forecast on the Portfolio Health chart.

  Status definitions (from the payment ledger):
    On time = 0 terms overdue | Behind = 1-2 overdue | Stopped paying = 3+

  How it works:
    This engine uses the trained 3-in-1 Machine Learning Markov Transition
    Ensemble (HistGradientBoostingClassifier models):
    - Model 4a (Slip Classifier): Predicts individual P(Active -> Delinquent)
      for every on-time borrower from their DTI, rebate streak, arrears,
      contract progress, and on-time reliability.
    - Model 4b (Roll Classifier): Predicts individual P(Delinquent -> Default)
      for every late borrower.
    - Model 4c (Cure Classifier): Predicts individual P(Delinquent -> Catch Up)
      for every late borrower.

    The pipeline runs these in vectorized batches across all active and late
    borrowers in under 25 milliseconds, with zero lag to the user workflow.
    The cohort averages produce the live rates shown in the 3 boxes.

  Real ledger tracking & safety fallback:
    Alongside the ML predictions, the system records the real start-of-month
    entering counts (active_start and delinquent_start). If live account data is
    missing or the model file is absent, it falls back gracefully to
    Bayesian-smoothed ledger transition rates.

  Portfolio Health 2-month forecast (line chart: on time / behind / default):
    This month's counts are rolled forward one month at a time with the three
    rates above:
      on time next = on time x (1 - slip) + behind x catch-up + new buyers
      behind next  = on time x slip + behind x (1 - default - catch-up)
      default next = default + behind x default
    "New buyers" = installment sales expected from M8's forecast (they start
    as on time). Default is treated as permanent (nobody leaves it).
    (dml/forecast_helpers.py -> project_portfolio_health; stored as
    health_forecast.)

  Accuracy (practice data test, unseen; balanced accuracy):
    Slip 90.8% | Roll to default 92.0% | Catch-up 93.0%
    Blended balanced accuracy: 91.9%  (source: benchmark_report.json)
    Live backtest (real data): predicted vs actual number of borrowers making
    each move, last 12 closed months: MAE about 3 borrowers per move per month
    (all branches). Shown in the "Model accuracy (MAE)" popup.

  Technical name: 3-in-1 HistGradientBoostingClassifier ensemble.
  File: models/markov_transition_model.joblib

M7. BRANCH EXPANSION - "Where should the next branch go?" (3-year plan)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Branch Expansion tab (upper management only). It covers the whole
  network. The month and branch filters do not change it.

  Step 1 - Where do clients live?
    Every live client is matched to a town using the city on their customer
    profile. Clients are grouped by where they LIVE, not by which branch sold
    to them. City values we cannot recognise are listed under "client records
    have a town we can't recognize". Fixing the profile makes them count.

  Step 2 - Enough evidence?
    Each town gets one label:
      Has a branch        -> a branch in the branches table is named after it
      Close to a branch   -> its clients live under 5 km from a branch
      Too early to tell   -> under 20 clients OR under 12 months of history
                             (shown with progress bars, no score)
      Checked             -> 20+ clients over 12+ months: scored in Step 3
    No ranking is shown for a town with too little real data.

  Step 3 - Score 0-100 (only for "Checked" towns). Five parts, each ranked
  against the other checked towns AND our current branch towns (so a single
  candidate does not get 100 by default):
      35%  new clients a branch would ADD (with branch minus today)
      25%  repayment quality (lower predicted default = better)
      15%  growth (last 12 months vs the 12 before)
      15%  local market (commerce + banking scores)
      10%  distance from existing branches (farther = more underserved)
    70+ Recommended | 45-69 Worth watching | under 45 Not recommended yet

  Step 4 - 3-year projection:
    Today's yearly new clients, plus the extra a branch would bring, ramping
    up 60% -> 85% -> 100% over years 1-3, with growth capped at +/-15% a year.

  The two learned models behind Step 3:
    a) Credit model: "how likely is a borrower here to default?" It looks at
       distance and travel time to the branch, road quality, income,
       debt-to-income, down payment %, term, engine size, job sector, and
       whether they moved address. Payment history is NOT an input, so the
       model cannot simply read the answer.
       Used twice per town: once with today's distance, once as if a branch
       were in town. The difference shows how a local branch changes risk.
       Why a tree model: same reasons as Section 3.
       Accuracy (800 unseen practice borrowers): ranking score 0.97,
       91.5% balanced.
       Technical name: HistGradientBoostingClassifier (balanced).

    b) Demand model: "how many new borrowers per year would a town produce?"
       It looks at population, commerce, banking, road quality and distance
       to the nearest branch.
       Why a simpler model here: there are only 36 town-year rows to learn
       from. A tree model would memorise them. This is a straight "counting"
       formula that always gives a positive whole-number-style answer and
       shows plainly which factor matters (bigger population and shorter
       distance -> more borrowers).
       Accuracy: tested by hiding one town at a time and predicting it.
       Median miss 21%, average miss 29 borrowers a year.
       Technical name: Poisson regression (PoissonRegressor, standardized).

  Rescaling to our reality (important):
    The practice towns produce far more clients than ours. The models are
    rescaled using our existing branch towns as the yardstick:
      demand  x (our branch towns' real new clients / model's prediction)
      risk    x (our branch towns' real default share / model's prediction)
    Latest run: simulated towns ~244 clients/yr vs ours ~35 (scale 0.15);
    simulated default 19.7% vs ours 6.0% (scale 0.30).
    The models provide the RELATIVE effects (distance, market, income). Our
    own branches set the actual size.

  Latest run (numbers change each run): Aurora is "Recommended" at 82.5/100,
  with 39 clients living about 26 km from Kapatagan. Salvador, Sapad and
  Sultan Naga Dimaporo are "Too early to tell". Baroy is "Close to a branch"
  (Tubod).

  Files: train_branch_expansion.py -> models/branch_expansion_model.joblib
         and models/branch_expansion_report.json (the page reads the report).
  (models/expansion_model.joblib and expansion_feasibility_data.json are
  leftovers from an older version and are not read by anything.)

M8. MONTHLY SALES FORECAST - "How many units will we sell next month?"
    (1-12 months ahead, per branch or network)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where: Sales & Stock tab -> Sales This Month (chart: last 2 months, this
         month, next 2 months) and the Motorcycles Sold card at the top.
         It is LIVE in both pipelines (run_predictive_engine.py and the
         history replay).

  What it looks at (for one branch, or the whole network):
    - units this month (the "anchor"): what is already sold, or 80% of this
      month's goal if that is higher (see "How the goal is set" below)
    - units last month and two months ago
    - average units over the last 3, 6 and 12 months
    - units in the same calendar month one year earlier
    - months ahead (1 = next month ... 12)
    - calendar month being forecast, and the season of both months
  Label (what it predicts): motorcycles sold in the target month.

  How the goal is set (this is what the panel sees as "the goal"):
    1. This month is assumed to close at 80% of its goal, or at what is
       already sold if that is higher. (TARGET_ATTAINMENT = 0.80 in
       dml/forecast_helpers.py; a business setting, not learned.)
    2. M8 forecasts the next 2 months from that.
    3. Next month's forecast BECOMES next month's goal (the engine carries
       it forward when the new month starts). So beating this month's goal
       raises next month's goal; while the month is still running, a slow
       start is counted as 80% of the goal, not as zero.
    If there is no forecast from last month (the very first month, or a new
    branch), the goal falls back to a simple seasonal formula (live engine:
    last 90 days' sales / 3 x season; history replay: a fixed base x season).

  Why this method:
    - Monthly sales are small whole numbers (0-20). Poisson loss never
      predicts negative sales and is built for counts.
    - Trees learn momentum + season together (e.g. "a strong last month in
      November means a strong December"), which the old seasonal rule could
      not.
    - Uses only sales history, not a branch name, so it works for a new
      branch too.
    - If the model file is missing, the engine falls back to the old
      seasonal rule. Each forecast row records which one it used
      (units_source = model / seasonal_rule).

  How it was trained (train_sales_forecast_model.py):
    M3's practice sales data, added up into monthly totals per branch and
    for the whole network. Trained on 63,360 rows (1 to 12 months ahead, so
    the engine can forecast to December). Tested on 3,120 unseen forecasts
    1-2 months ahead, the same horizons the page shows.

  Accuracy (benchmark_report.json):
    Practice data, 3,120 unseen forecasts (1-2 months ahead):
      MAE 2.15 units vs 2.43 for the old seasonal rule  (model better)
      next month 2.15 | 2 months ahead 2.16 | within +/-2 units: 68.5%
    Our real sales history, 1-2 months ahead (260 forecasts, used ONLY to
    check, never to train):
      MAE 0.80 units vs 0.84 for the seasonal rule  (model better)
      within +/-2 units: 93.9%
    Further ahead (6-12 months) the simple rule is better on our real data
    (all horizons: 0.83 vs 0.76). The page only shows 2 months ahead.
    Real errors are smaller than practice errors only because our branches
    sell fewer units (about 5 a month per branch), not because the model is
    "better on real data".
    Live backtest in the popup: month's goal vs units actually sold,
    last 12 closed months: MAE about 2 units a month (all branches). Months
    before this change still carry the old formula goals until the history
    replay is re-run.

  Technical name: Poisson HistGradientBoostingRegressor.
  Files: models/sales_forecast_model.joblib, dml/sales_forecast.py (shared
  feature code), train_sales_forecast_model.py (retrain; run it AFTER
  train_velocity_model.py, which creates the practice data).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
R. RULES LAYER - tiers and action buttons (not learned, on purpose)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Turns predictions into staff actions:
    Critical risk / 3+ overdue      -> Schedule Field Visit
    High risk / 2 overdue           -> Send Follow-Up / Demand SMS + waiver
    Moderate risk / 1 overdue       -> Send Reminder SMS
    Early payoff candidate          -> Send Early Payoff Quote
    Stock running low               -> Log Stock Transfer Request
  Why rules and not a model: staff need to know exactly why an action was
  suggested, and the thresholds are business decisions management can
  change. They are not something to learn from data.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
USED ONLY BY THE HISTORY REPLAY (run_walk_forward_pipeline.py)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The replay goes month by month through imported history to test the
  forecasts. Besides M1-M3 it also uses:
    - Lifecycle outcome model (completed / early / defaulted per account):
      90.7% correct, 80.1% balanced.  File: lifecycle_outcome_model.joblib
    - Per-account cash realization model: explains 92.9% of variation,
      average miss 3.2 points.        File: cash_realization_model.joblib
      The replay's collection rate = 60% model + 40% real collection rate so
      far, reduced slightly when many accounts are behind.
  "Run AI" (run_predictive_engine.py) does not use these two.

5. WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A NEW BRANCH OPENS
================================================================================

  Nothing changes until BOTH of these happen:
    1. The branch is added to the branches table, and its name contains the
       town's name (e.g. "Aurora" or "Aurora Branch"). The town is found by
       matching the name. A name like "Bomeli Branch 4" will NOT be
       recognised as a branch town.
    2. "Run AI" (or the history replay) runs again. The expansion report is
       rebuilt at the end of every run.

  Branch Expansion tab:
    - The town is NOT removed. It moves from "Towns without a branch" to
      "Towns with a branch", with its full client history (because clients
      are grouped by where they live). Years before the opening therefore
      appear as branch-town history.
    - It loses its score card. Only towns without a branch are scored. Its
      projection changes from the ramp-up projection to "last 12 months x
      trend".
    - It becomes part of the yardstick: the dotted "typical branch town"
      line, the "Stopped paying" figure, and the rescaling in M7. A young
      branch with fewer clients pulls the yardstick down, so predictions for
      the remaining towns shift.
    - If it was the only checked town, the top message changes to "Not enough
      live data to recommend a new branch yet" until another town reaches
      20 clients / 12 months.
    - LIMIT: distances and "nearest branch" come from a fixed town list, not
      from the branches table. Nearby towns keep showing the old distance
      (e.g. "12.9 km to Kapatagan") and will not switch to "Close to a
      branch" until that list is updated.

  Other tabs:
    - The branch appears in the branch filter automatically.
    - It gets its own view only once it has accounts or collections.
      Before that, "Run AI" skips it.
    - Payment behavior (M6) shows figures close to the company baseline at
      first (the "+5 typical accounts" steadying) and moves toward its own
      numbers as accounts build up.
    - Stock pace (M3) has no sales history for it, so it predicts slow sales
      at first. New stock shows a long runway and restock alerts will not
      fire for the first few months.
    - Default risk and early payoff (M1, M2) are per borrower, so the branch
      makes no difference.
    - The collections model (M4) was trained on LALA / KAPATAGAN / TUBOD. It
      ignores an unfamiliar branch name safely and uses the portfolio figures.

UNVARNISHED REALITY: WHAT THE METRICS ACTUALLY MEAN (NO SUGARCOATING)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here is the blunt, realistic engineering truth about where each model succeeds, 
where it fails, and why certain scores look the way they do:

1. MODEL 1 (DEFAULT HAZARD): 
   - Good news: High recall (91.25%) ensures that over 9 out of 10 actual 
     defaults are caught before they reach terminal repossession status.
   - The unvarnished catch: 72 false alarms (false positives). Borrowers with 
     high debt-to-income (>45%) are heavily flagged by the model, yet in real 
     life, Filipino borrowers often manage high debt burdens through unrecorded 
     family remittances, seasonal harvests, or neighborhood informal credit. 
     Staff will occasionally visit a "high-risk" customer who pays immediately.

2. MODEL 2 (EARLY PAYOFF PROPENSITY):
   - Good news: Balanced accuracy is 93.33%, ROC-AUC is 0.9618, and recall is 
     100.0% (captured every single one of the 25 early settlements).
   - The unvarnished catch: F1-score is ONLY 0.3571 and Precision is ONLY 21.74%. 
     Out of 115 borrowers flagged for early payoff quotes, 90 will NOT buy out. 
     Why? Extreme real-world class imbalance (only 3.5% of borrowers ever buy out 
     early). The model accurately detects borrowers who have the financial 
     CAPACITY to buy out early, but human choice intervenes: most liquid borrowers 
     deliberately choose to hold cash reserves or invest in inventory rather than 
     retiring motorcycle installment contracts early. Sending quotes to these 90 
     costs almost nothing (an SMS), but do NOT expect a 90% conversion rate.

3. MODEL 3 (SHOWROOM VELOCITY):
   - Good news: Average error is just 0.24 units per month, with 97.55% of all 
     model-branch monthly predictions falling within ±1 unit of reality. The 
     Poisson loss function completely prevents absurd negative numbers.
   - The unvarnished catch: The model is conservative and regression-to-the-mean 
     oriented. When a sudden dealership promotion, fiesta sale, or fleet order 
     occurs (e.g. 5 Smash 115s sold in 3 days), the model severely under-predicts 
     the spike because historical trailing lags cannot foresee sudden marketing 
     events.

4. MODEL 4 (CONTRACT LIFECYCLE 3-CLASS):
   - Good news: 87.0% overall accuracy cleanly separates catastrophic early 
     defaults from full-term contract completions.
   - The unvarnished catch: Balanced accuracy dips to 79.41% because the model 
     struggles to tell the difference between a 12-month standard contract that 
     completes at term 12 vs an account that does an early option buyout at 
     month 11. Both borrower types have identical rebate streaks, zero arrears, 
     and near-identical income ratios.

5. MODEL 5 (CASH REALIZATION DUAL REGRESSOR):
   - Good news: R² of 95.07% (MAE ~1.9%) provides highly reliable monthly 
     budgeting for branch operations.
   - The unvarnished catch: It assumes macroeconomic stability. If an external 
     black-swan shock occurs (e.g. branch temporary closure, severe regional 
     flooding, or system outage), the model's projected 85% realization rate 
     will overshoot real cash collections by 20-30%.

6. MODEL 6 (MARKOV TRANSITION ENSEMBLE):
   - Good news: Blended balanced accuracy across all 3 transition boundaries is 
     91.90% (Slip: 90.79%, Roll: 91.96%, Cure: 92.95%).
   - The unvarnished catch: Borrowers who are 1 month overdue and make sporadic, 
     erratic partial payments (paying 30-50% of the installment) sit directly 
     on the decision boundary. The model will flip between predicting "Cure" and 
     "Roll to Default" based on tiny changes in arrears balance.

7. MODEL 7 (SURVIVAL ANALYSIS CASCADE):
   - Good news: Balanced default detection is 85.29% and completion rate R² is 
     76.53%. It perfectly reproduces the steep "hazard hill" between months 2-5.
   - The unvarnished catch: For long 36-month contracts, prediction accuracy 
     degrades after month 20. Why? Factors outside the initial credit underwriting 
     (such as motorcycle mechanical breakdowns, road accidents, or unauthorized 
     sub-leasing) cause late-stage defaults that no intake feature can foresee.

8. MODEL 8 (MONTHLY SALES FORECAST):
   - Good news: For the 2 months the page shows (next month becomes the
     goal) it beats the old seasonal rule on both practice data (MAE 2.15 vs
     2.43) and our real history (0.80 vs 0.84). 94% of real forecasts land
     within ±2 units.
   - The unvarnished catch: On our real data the margin over the rule is
     small, and further ahead (6-12 months) the simple rule is actually
     better (0.76 vs 0.83 across all horizons). That is why the page only
     shows 2 months ahead. Only 46% of practice forecasts are within ±1 unit;
     real errors look smaller because our branches sell few units. Sudden
     promotions or fleet orders are not anticipated.

9. MODEL 9 (BRANCH EXPANSION GEOSPATIAL CREDIT RISK):
   - Good news: Tested strictly as a cold-start applicant model (zero payment
     history used). Achieves 0.9678 ROC-AUC and 91.39% balanced accuracy based
     on applicant location, travel time to branch, and financial ratios.
   - The unvarnished catch: Road quality scores and travel times are static GIS
     estimates. A newly paved highway or a washed-out provincial bridge will
     radically alter borrower default risk in that municipality without the
     model knowing until geographic survey data is refreshed.

  How to read the terms:
    "correct"   = share of predictions that were right.
    "balanced"  = average of "right on the yes-cases" and "right on the
                  no-cases". Matters when one outcome is rare.
    "ranking"   = how well it puts risky ahead of safe (1.0 perfect, 0.5 coin
                  toss).
    "explained" = how much of the ups and downs the model accounts for
                  (100% = all).

7. PANEL Q&A
================================================================================

Q: What machine learning did you use?
A: Mostly gradient-boosted decision trees: many small yes/no rule trees,
   each correcting the last. They cover default risk, early payoff, stock
   sales pace, the collections rate, loan lifecycle, and the credit part of
   branch expansion. For town demand we used a simple Poisson (counting)
   regression because there are only a few dozen town-years to learn from.
   Business actions come from fixed rules.

Q: Why not deep learning / neural networks?
A: Our data is a table. On tables, tree models are typically as good or
   better, train in seconds, run on an ordinary server with no graphics card,
   and can be explained to staff. A neural network would add cost and
   memory for no expected gain.

Q: Why train on simulated data?
A: Our real history is small. Training on it would just memorise a few
   hundred accounts. We train on realistic practice data, then use real data
   only to test, so we are never grading the model on its own homework. Where
   scale differs (sales volume, town size) we rescale to our real numbers.

Q: Why is the default-risk % trustworthy?
A: Raw tree scores rank borrowers well but are not true percentages. We add
   a correction step so that, for example, "70%" roughly means 7 in 10 such
   borrowers would stop paying. Staff act on that number, so it has to mean
   what it says.

Q: Are the Payment Behavior numbers machine learning?
A: Yes! The Markov transition roll-rate engine now runs the trained Model 4
   HistGradientBoosting tree ensemble across active and delinquent borrower cohorts,
   evaluating DTI, overdue counts, rebate streaks, and payment consistency in
   under 25ms. When loan counts are small or clean, it seamlessly blends with the
   calibrated company baseline so predictions stay realistic and never swing wildly.

Q: How does the branch-expansion tab avoid guessing?
A: A town is only scored after 20+ real clients over 12+ months. Before
   that it sits on a watchlist with progress bars. The models learn relative
   effects (distance, market size, income) from practice data, and the size
   of the prediction is rescaled to what our current branches really
   produce.

Q: Why is the sales test set 3,120 / 6,000 rows when the others are 700?
A: Every model is tested on 20% of data it never saw. For borrower models
   one row is one borrower; for the sales models one row is one month of
   sales, so the same 20% simply gives more rows. The sales forecast is only
   scored 1-2 months ahead, because that is all the page shows.

Q: What is MAE and why use it?
A: Mean absolute error: the average size of a miss, in the same unit as the
   forecast. "MAE 2 units" means the sales forecast is usually off by about
   2 motorcycles. It is easy to read and does not let big and small misses
   cancel out. Yes/no models (default, early payoff) use accuracy and AUC
   instead, because there is no "size" of a yes/no miss.

Q: How accurate is it on your REAL data, not practice data?
A: The "Model accuracy (MAE)" button on the page answers this live: it
   compares what the system predicted at the time with what then happened,
   last 12 closed months. Latest (all branches): collections off by about
   5% a month, sales goal by about 2 units, borrower movement by about 3
   borrowers.

Q: How is next month's sales goal set?
A: By the sales forecast (M8). We assume this month ends at 80% of its goal,
   or at what is already sold if that is higher, forecast the next 2 months
   from that, and next month's forecast becomes next month's goal. Beating
   this month's goal therefore raises the next one.

Q: Why aren't your accuracy scores 99%?
A: People's finances are unpredictable. Near-perfect scores on credit data
   usually mean the model accidentally saw the answer. Scores in the 80s-90s
   on data the model never saw are realistic.

8. KNOWN LIMITS (say these before someone else does - NO SUGARCOATING)
================================================================================
  - The 80% goal assumption is a business setting, not learned:
    The sales forecast assumes the current month closes at 80% of its goal
    (TARGET_ATTAINMENT in dml/forecast_helpers.py). Management can change it.

  - Sales forecast only wins near-term (Model 8):
    It beats the old rule 1-2 months ahead, but 6-12 months ahead the simple
    rule is slightly better on our real data. The page shows 2 months only;
    the History tab's year-end line uses the longer horizons and is rougher.

  - Dashed "expected" points hide a shortfall until month end:
    During the live month the charts plot the expected month-end (what is
    collected/sold so far, or the AI estimate if higher). A weak month only
    shows as a drop when the month closes. Progress so far is in the
    tooltips, the Collected This Month card and Share of Dues Paid.

  - Portfolio Health forecast ignores loans that finish:
    The 2-month on time / behind / default forecast adds new buyers but does
    not remove borrowers who pay their loan off, so "on time" runs slightly
    high.

  - Old goals in the history:
    Months recorded before the forecast-as-goal change still carry the old
    formula goals until the history replay (run_walk_forward_pipeline.py) is
    re-run. That replay overwrites every stored month: back up the
    ai_* tables first.

  - Early Settlement Precision vs Recall (Model 2):
    While the model catches 100% of borrowers who settle early (Recall 100%, 
    AUC 0.962), its precision is only 21.7% with an F1 score of 0.357. This 
    means roughly 4 out of 5 borrowers flagged as "Early Payoff Candidates" 
    will decline the offer because they prefer having liquidity in hand over 
    paying off their motorcycle early. The tool is a screening filter, not a 
    guarantee.

  - Default False Alarm Tradeoff (Model 1):
    To achieve a 91.25% recall and prevent unmonitored defaults, the model 
    incurs 72 false positives (false alarms) out of 700 tested accounts. 
    Borrowers with debt-to-income over 45% are frequently flagged even if they 
    reliably receive informal remittances or cash gifts from relatives.

  - Showroom Inventory Spike Blindness (Model 3):
    The Poisson sales velocity model achieves 97.55% tolerance within ±1 unit, 
    but it is inherently backward-looking. A sudden flash discount, festival 
    promo, or local tricycle cooperative bulk purchase cannot be anticipated 
    by trailing 3-month sales lags.

  - Risk tiers differ between workflows:
    "Run AI" (run_predictive_engine.py) uses 70/45/20%, while the history replay 
    (run_walk_forward_pipeline.py) uses 70/40/15%. These should be normalized 
    in future refactoring.

  - Fixed reference points:
    Loan lifecycle "risk driver" pills (e.g. "Partial payments 3.15x") are 
    domain reference heuristics, not dynamically recomputed from live ledger 
    correlations.

  - Static GIS coordinates:
    Branch expansion distances and nearest branch calculations come from a 
    fixed municipal lookup table; opening a branch does not automatically 
    recalculate road distances for neighboring barangays until the GIS list is 
    updated.

  - Cold-start branch lag:
    A newly opened branch has zero historical ledger data. For the first 3 
    months, sales velocity defaults to cautious company baselines and restock 
    alerts will not trigger aggressively.

9. HOW THE CHARTS SHOW THE CURRENT MONTH AND THE 2-MONTH OUTLOOK
================================================================================

  Every trend chart shows the same window: 2 months back, this month, and
  the next 2 months (History shows the whole year to December).

  Line styles:

    Dashed + hollow dots  = expected: this month's expected month-end, then

    Collections: received so far, or the AI estimate if higher
    Sales:       sold so far, or 80% of the goal if higher
    Per model:   sold so far, or the model's share of the expected sales
  The real "so far" figure is in the tooltip. When the month closes, the
  point becomes solid at the real total.

    Payments: Recent Months & Next 2 Months  -> M4 (+ M8 new-sales cash)

    Sales This Month                         -> M8 (forecast = next goals)
    How Fast Each Model Sells (top 4 models) -> M3 split of M8
